\section{Introduction}
\label{intro}

\subsection{Motivation}

Online recruitment fraud is the posting of fake job advertisements, usually to collect money or
personal data from the people who answer them~\cite{vidros2017}. For a job board,
every fake ad that stays online harms an applicant and erodes the trust of the real employers
that pay for the service. Moderators cannot read every incoming ad, so the practical need is a
model that \emph{ranks} new ads by how likely they are to be fake, so that the moderators' limited
time goes to the top of that list.

\subsection{Problem statement}

We study this task on EMSCAD, the Employment Scam Aegean Dataset~\cite{vidros2017},
which contains 17,880 job ads labelled real or fraudulent by hand. The task is binary text
classification, but two properties of the data make it harder than it looks. First, only 866 ads
(4.84\%) are fraudulent: a model that calls every ad real already reaches 95.2\% accuracy while
catching nothing. Second, many ads are copies or near-copies of each other, so a careless random
split puts the same text in both the training and the test rows and rewards memorisation.

These two properties also explain why published EMSCAD results are hard to compare. They range
from 91\% to 99.9\% accuracy, but they use different splits, metrics and resampling schemes, and
at least one was measured on a resampled test set~\cite{taneja2025,amaar2022}.
Most report a single split, which says nothing about how much a score would move on another one.

\subsection{Objectives and approach}

The project asks one question: \emph{under a single protocol that does not leak, how do classical
text models, pretrained transformers and label-free detectors compare on EMSCAD, and what do they
rely on?} To answer it, we first explore the data and let each finding fix a design decision
(Section~\ref{data}). These decisions become one evaluation protocol shared by every model:
duplicate-aware grouped splits, five split seeds, PR-AUC as the main metric, a threshold chosen on
validation and a test split scored only once (Section~\ref{protocol}). Within this protocol we
compare a lineup that covers the course, from a dummy baseline through linear models, a decision
tree, an MLP and Naive Bayes to a fine-tuned DistilBERT, a zero-shot language model and an
unsupervised one-class SVM (Section~\ref{models}). Finally, we combine the strongest models of two
families by stacking and examine what the models rely on (Sections~\ref{results}
and~\ref{explain}).

\subsection{Summary of findings}

A logistic regression on BM25-weighted words is the best single model (test PR-AUC 0.847 over
five seeds), and a fine-tuned DistilBERT does not beat it (0.816). The two make different
mistakes, and a stack of the two reaches 0.878, better than either on every seed. Detectors that
never see a fraud label stay near the 0.049 floor. The explanations show that part of what the
models learn is a property of how EMSCAD was collected rather than of fraud itself.

\subsection{Report structure}

Section~\ref{related} reviews earlier work. Section~\ref{data} explores the data and derives the
design decisions, which Section~\ref{protocol} turns into a preprocessing pipeline and an
evaluation protocol. Section~\ref{models} presents the models and why each is included,
Section~\ref{results} the results, and Section~\ref{explain} what the models rely on and where they
fail. Section~\ref{discussion} interprets the results and lists the limitations, and
Section~\ref{conclusion} concludes.

\textbf{Code.} Four notebooks run the project in order: \texttt{01} EDA and preprocessing,
\texttt{02} classical models, \texttt{03} deep models (GPU server only) and \texttt{04} analysis.
Shared logic lives in \texttt{code/modules/} and is covered by unit tests. Every number in this
report is read from the result files the notebooks save; \texttt{report/make\_figures.py} builds
the figures and tables from them.
